1. What regression is for

A delivery team wants to estimate arrival time before a van leaves a warehouse. A manufacturer wants to predict energy consumption from production volume and temperature. A marketplace wants to forecast the sale price of an item. Each task asks for a numerical quantity, rather than a class label. Regression is a family of methods for learning this relationship from observed examples.

The output need not be unbounded or perfectly continuous. A count is numerical too, although a count model may represent it better than an unconstrained straight line. What matters is that the prediction and its error have a meaningful numerical interpretation. Predicting a delivery time of 42 minutes when the trip takes 45 minutes creates a three-minute error. Predicting class number 4 instead of class number 1 does not automatically create a meaningful distance of three.

This tutorial develops the vocabulary needed for the series: features, targets, predictions, residuals, loss, and generalization. You will derive a constant baseline, calculate an error by hand, and run a small training-and-testing experiment. Prerequisites are arithmetic, averages, and basic Python arrays. Later tutorials introduce calculus and linear algebra where they are needed.

2. A prediction is a decision under uncertainty

Imagine collecting delivery records. Distance is an input feature; elapsed time is the target. Two trips of the same distance can take different times because traffic, loading delays, and driver behaviour vary. A regression model does not remove this uncertainty. It estimates an aspect of the conditional distribution of time given the available inputs.

With squared-error loss, the population-optimal prediction is the conditional mean. With absolute-error loss, a conditional median is optimal. These can differ substantially for a skewed distribution. If most deliveries take 30 minutes but occasional traffic jams create two-hour journeys, the mean is pulled upward more strongly than the median. The choice of loss therefore encodes which errors the prediction should care about.

A useful modelling workflow begins before choosing an algorithm. Specify when a prediction will be made, which fields exist at that time, what outcome is measured, and which mistake is costly. “Final delivery status” is not a legitimate feature for a prediction made before dispatch. A highly accurate model using that field would solve the wrong information problem.

3. Features, targets, and risk

Let there be n observations. For observation i, the vector of p input features is xi, the observed target is yi, and the model prediction is f(xi). The residual is observed minus predicted. Residuals are quantities computed from a fitted model; the unobserved noise in a data-generating process is a different object.

\hat y_i=f(\mathbf x_i),\qquad e_i=y_i-\hat y_i,\qquad \widehat R(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-f(\mathbf x_i))^2

Here a hat marks a prediction or an empirical estimate. The empirical risk R-hat is mean squared error on the observations used in the sum. Squaring removes the residual's sign and makes larger errors disproportionately expensive. It also changes the unit: minutes become squared minutes. Taking a square root produces RMSE in minutes.

A model can achieve low empirical risk by memorizing its training observations. Our actual aim is small expected loss on future examples from the intended deployment setting. That expected loss is not directly available during training. A carefully separated test set gives an estimate, subject to sampling variation and the relevance of its distribution.

4. Why the mean is the squared-error baseline

Suppose the model ignores every feature and predicts the same number c for each observation. We can derive the best c rather than guess it. Define J(c) as the average squared distance from the targets. Differentiate term by term, set the derivative to zero, and solve.

J(c)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(y_i-c)^2,\quad \frac{dJ}{dc}=\frac{2}{n}\sum_i(c-y_i)=2(c-\bar y)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad c=\bar y

The symbol y-bar denotes the arithmetic mean of the targets. The second derivative is 2, which is positive, so the stationary point is the unique global minimum. This result explains the mean-predicting dummy regressor and the reference value used by R². The training mean must be computed on the training set; computing it on the test targets would leak evaluation information.

The population version uses a random target Y and a fixed feature value x. Write m(x) for the conditional mean. Expanding a square around m(x), the cross term vanishes because the conditional mean of Y − m(x) is zero.

\mathbb E[(Y-a)^2\mid X=x]=\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x)+(m(x)-a)^2

The candidate prediction is a. The first term is irreducible conditional variance for these inputs; changing a cannot remove it. The second term is minimized by a = m(x). This identity assumes the conditional second moment exists. It does not say that a finite fitted model recovers the true mean, or that collecting additional informative features cannot reduce uncertainty.

6. A complete numerical example

Take a three-row dataset: distance x = [1, 2, 3] and time y = [2, 3, 7]. The mean target is (2 + 3 + 7)/3 = 4. The constant model predicts [4, 4, 4], with residuals [−2, −1, 3]. Its squared errors are [4, 1, 9], so MSE = 14/3 and RMSE is approximately 2.160.

Now consider the candidate line y-hat = 0 + 2x. It predicts [2, 4, 6]. The residuals become [0, −1, 1], squared errors [0, 1, 1], and MSE = 2/3. MAE is also 2/3; RMSE is approximately 0.816. For x = 4, the line predicts 8. We cannot calculate that future prediction's error until its true target is observed.

The line improves the training fit, but three in-sample points do not establish future performance. The chosen candidate is not necessarily the ordinary least-squares optimum either. Separating “a model,” “the optimizer of a stated objective,” and “a model that generalizes” prevents many misunderstandings.

8. Strengths, limits, and applications

Regression makes numerical trade-offs explicit. Residuals can be inspected in physical units, baselines are straightforward, and simple models are inexpensive to fit. A single-feature least-squares fit can be calculated in linear time in the sample count. More flexible estimators can represent richer relationships, but flexibility does not fix mislabeled targets or a mismatched evaluation design.

Do not interpret a predictive coefficient as a causal effect without an appropriate identification argument. Distance may predict time, but an observational relationship between advertising spend and revenue may be confounded by seasonal demand. A regression can forecast effectively while giving a misleading answer to an intervention question.

Extrapolation is another limitation. A model trained on short trips may predict implausible times for a journey across a continent. Monitor the input ranges, the temporal distribution of errors, and important subgroups. When the cost of late deliveries is asymmetric, consider a quantile-oriented objective rather than assuming a mean prediction matches the business decision.

Turn a vague goal into an experiment

Suppose a warehouse asks for “better delivery predictions.” Convert that into a contract before collecting a training table. The unit of observation might be a dispatched parcel, the timestamp might be departure from the warehouse, and the target might be elapsed minutes until the first delivery attempt. An undelivered parcel has an unresolved outcome rather than an ordinary zero-minute delivery. Decide how such cases are represented before training, because excluding them can change the population being modelled.

Next define the baseline at the same prediction timestamp. A global training mean is a useful mechanical check, but an operational baseline might use a route-specific historical median. Compare the new model with the system it would actually replace, not only with an artificially weak reference. A route-specific baseline also needs a fallback for unseen routes and must calculate its statistics without evaluation targets.

Separate estimation error from measurement error. If the recorded delivery timestamp is rounded to the nearest hour, a minute-level error metric may suggest precision that the labels do not support. If different teams record different event definitions, a flexible model can learn those recording practices instead of the intended phenomenon. A label audit can be more valuable than adding another estimator.

Understand uncertainty before promising precision

A point prediction summarizes a distribution. Two routes can both have a predicted mean of 40 minutes while one is almost always between 35 and 45 and the other ranges from 10 to 90. The same mean supports very different operational decisions. A prediction interval aims to describe uncertainty about a future observation; a confidence interval for the mean describes uncertainty about an estimated average relationship. They are not interchangeable.

Neither interval can be added honestly by taking an arbitrary percentage around the point prediction. Its construction needs assumptions or a calibration method and an evaluation of coverage on appropriate data. The first task in this introductory series is to make the point-prediction workflow trustworthy. Once that is established, uncertainty estimation can become a separate, testable extension with an explicit coverage target and a documented population.

9. Interview questions

1. What distinguishes regression from classification?

Regression predicts a numerical quantity with meaningful numerical error. Classification predicts membership or class probabilities. Numeric encoding alone does not make a classification label a regression target.

2. Why start with a dummy model?

It quantifies how much predictive value the features add beyond a simple training-derived reference. A complex model that cannot beat a relevant baseline deserves investigation.

3. Why does squared loss target the conditional mean?

Its conditional expected loss decomposes into conditional variance plus the squared distance between the candidate prediction and the conditional mean. Only the second term depends on the candidate.

4. Are residuals the same as noise?

No. Noise is unobserved variation around a specified data-generating mean. Residuals use an estimated model and can include misspecification, parameter error, and noise.

5. Can training error be zero?

Yes. An interpolating model may fit every training target exactly. That says little by itself about future error.

6. Must regression targets be normally distributed?

No. Fitting a squared-error predictor does not require normally distributed targets. Classical exact small-sample inference introduces assumptions about conditional errors, not marginal target normality.

7. What is leakage?

Using information unavailable at prediction time or allowing evaluation data to influence model fitting and selection. Even unsupervised preprocessing can leak information if learned before the split.

8. How should temporal observations be split?

Usually along time, with training before evaluation and any necessary gap. A random split can let future information or near-duplicates inflate the apparent performance.

10. Practice and key takeaways

  • Change the synthetic noise scale. Compare test error with how accurately the fitted slope recovers the generating coefficient.
  • Replace the mean dummy model with a median predictor. Evaluate both with MAE and explain the difference.
  • Build a delivery-time mini-project with an explicit prediction timestamp. Write down which columns are legitimate inputs before fitting anything.
  • Calculate the candidate line's R² for the worked dataset, using the target mean 4 as the reference. You should obtain 1 − 2/14 = 6/7.

The main lesson is to define the prediction problem before selecting its estimator. A numerical target, an explicit loss, a credible baseline, and a defensible evaluation split are the foundations on which the rest of this series builds.
