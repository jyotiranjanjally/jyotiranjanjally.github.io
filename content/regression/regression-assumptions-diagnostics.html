1. Separate four different questions

A regression can be computed even when its assumptions are poorly matched to the data. This creates confusion: one person says an assumption is “required,” while another points to a fitted model that ran successfully. They may be talking about different goals.

Ask four questions separately. Can the numerical objective be minimized? Do the coefficients estimate the intended conditional relationship? Are the reported standard errors and intervals justified? Do predictions work on the intended future population? Each question has different requirements and evidence.

This tutorial connects classical assumptions to their purposes, explains residual diagnostics without overclaiming, and demonstrates common engineering failures. You will calculate leverage and residual error for a small dataset and run a diagnostic script with controlled misspecification. Prerequisites are multiple regression and basic evaluation metrics.

2. State the data model precisely

\mathbf y=X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\qquad\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid X]=0

The n-by-k design matrix X includes an intercept, β is a fixed k-vector of coefficients in this classical formulation, and ε is unobserved error. Zero conditional mean says the model captures the conditional target mean using the chosen feature representation. It is stronger than the fitted residuals merely summing to zero.

Full column rank gives a unique OLS coefficient vector. Independent errors with common conditional variance are often summarized by Cov(ε | X) = σ²I. These assumptions support the familiar covariance formula and the Gauss–Markov efficiency result among linear unbiased estimators. Gaussian conditional errors provide exact finite-sample t and F inference when the remaining assumptions hold.

Neither normal predictors nor a normally distributed marginal target is required by this setup. A strongly skewed feature can be valid. What matters is the conditional structure and the intended inference. Conversely, a normal-looking residual histogram cannot rule out confounding, temporal leakage, or a badly chosen target.

3. Where bias and uncertainty enter

For a full-rank design, substitute y = Xβ + ε into the OLS solution. The result separates the true coefficient vector from the propagated error.

\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\boldsymbol\beta+(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\boldsymbol\varepsilon

Taking conditional expectation shows why zero conditional mean error matters: the second term then has expectation zero. If errors systematically depend on features, the expected coefficient error need not vanish. Adding more observations does not automatically correct this kind of misspecification.

\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\mid X)=(X^TX)^{-1}X^T\Omega X(X^TX)^{-1}

Here Ω = Cov(ε | X) is the error covariance matrix. Under Ω = σ²I, this reduces to σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹. Heteroscedasticity or correlation changes Ω, so the simple covariance expression may be wrong even when the coefficient estimator remains unbiased under zero conditional mean.

Robust covariance estimators address specific covariance assumptions. They do not repair omitted-variable bias, reverse a leaked train/test split, or make a nonrepresentative dataset representative. Cluster-aware or time-series-aware inference requires a covariance method matched to the dependence structure.

4. Read residual plots as evidence

A residual-versus-fitted plot can reveal curvature, changing spread, or extreme points. A U-shaped trend suggests missing nonlinear structure. A fan shape suggests changing conditional variance. Residuals ordered by time can reveal autocorrelation or drift that a shuffled scatterplot hides.

Residuals are not independent copies of the original errors. With hat matrix H = X(XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀ, fitted residuals are e = (I − H)ε under the model. Even if the original errors are independent and homoscedastic, residual variance depends on leverage.

h_{ii}=\mathbf x_i^T(X^TX)^{-1}\mathbf x_i,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(e_i\mid X)=\sigma^2(1-h_{ii})

The row vector xᵢ includes the intercept. The diagonal hᵢᵢ measures leverage: how unusual an observation's feature position is relative to the design. High leverage is about inputs, while a large residual is about prediction disagreement. An observation can have one without the other.

A normal Q–Q plot compares residual quantiles with a normal reference and can expose skew or heavy tails. It does not establish independence or zero conditional mean. On very large datasets, small deviations may be visually or statistically detectable without being operationally important; on small datasets, serious problems may be difficult to detect.

5. Calculate residuals and leverage

Use x = [0,1,2] and y = [0,1,4]. Their means are 1 and 5/3. The OLS slope is 2 and intercept −1/3, producing predictions [−1/3,5/3,11/3]. Residuals are [1/3,−2/3,1/3]; SSE = 2/3 and MSE = 2/9.

The endpoint residuals are positive while the middle residual is negative, a tiny example of curvature left by a straight line. Three observations are far too few for a strong diagnostic conclusion, but the arithmetic shows what a pattern means. At x = 3 the line predicts 17/3; if the underlying relationship is y = x², the true target is 9, yielding residual 10/3 and squared error 100/9.

For simple regression with an intercept, hᵢᵢ = 1/n + (xᵢ − x-bar)²/Σ(x − x-bar)². Here the denominator is 2. The endpoint leverages are 1/3 + 1/2 = 5/6, and the middle leverage is 1/3. Their sum is 2, equal to the number of fitted coefficients. High leverage at the endpoints explains why observations there can strongly affect the line.

7. A diagnostic workflow

The script generates a nonlinear signal with feature-dependent noise, fits a linear baseline, and plots held-out residuals against fitted values alongside actual versus predicted values. It calculates leverage on the training design separately. Keeping these roles distinct avoids treating a training influence diagnostic as an out-of-sample performance estimate.

When you see curvature in this example, you know its cause because the data-generating function is supplied. With real data, several explanations can fit the same visual pattern. Investigate data quality, feature definitions, and plausible alternative models before claiming a specific cause.

8. Common failures and their consequences

  • Preprocessing before splitting. A scaler, imputer, selector, or target encoder learned on all rows uses evaluation information. Put learned steps inside the validation pipeline.
  • Duplicate entities across partitions. Repeated records from one person or machine can make a random split easier than predicting genuinely new entities. Use an appropriate group split.
  • Future information. A rolling feature that accidentally includes future observations turns forecasting into retrospective reconstruction. Audit timestamps and window boundaries.
  • Outcome-dependent filtering. Dropping difficult test rows because their errors are large changes the evaluation population after seeing the answer.
  • Unchecked extrapolation. A coefficient fitted over a narrow range may yield impossible predictions far beyond it. Record ranges and deployment constraints.
  • Transforming the target without revisiting interpretation. A model of log target estimates a different conditional quantity. Exponentiating a predicted log mean does not generally recover the original-scale conditional mean.

Outliers deserve investigation, not automatic deletion. Some are data-entry errors; others are precisely the rare operational events the model must handle. Document any exclusion rule before final evaluation and apply it consistently to the population the system will actually serve.

Influence combines residual size and leverage

Leverage describes where a row sits in feature space, while influence asks how much a fitted result changes when that row is perturbed or removed. A common OLS influence summary is Cook's distance. With k fitted coefficients including the intercept, residual eᵢ, leverage hᵢᵢ, and estimated error variance s², its conventional expression is:

D_i=\frac{e_i^2}{k s^2}\frac{h_{ii}}{(1-h_{ii})^2}

This formula assumes the usual full-rank OLS setup and hᵢᵢ below one. It combines disagreement with the fitted model and the potential for an observation to move the fit. A large value is an investigation signal, not an automatic deletion rule. Threshold heuristics vary with sample size and purpose, and should not replace examination of the original record.

If a high-influence observation is valid and part of the deployment population, removing it can make the model less relevant. Consider whether a robust loss, a different functional form, or a separate population model is justified. Evaluate that changed procedure without choosing exclusions based on the final test score.

Multicollinearity affects interpretation before it breaks prediction

One diagnostic regresses each feature on the remaining features and computes a variance inflation factor, VIFⱼ = 1/(1 − Rⱼ²), where Rⱼ² is the auxiliary regression's in-sample coefficient of determination. A value near infinity indicates near-linear dependence. The formula describes inflation relative to an orthogonal design under a classical homoscedastic model, not a universal feature-removal command.

For Rⱼ² = 0.9, the VIF is 10. This means the feature's separate coefficient is much harder to estimate under that comparison. It does not establish that predictions are poor, that the feature is useless, or that deleting it improves generalization. Ridge may stabilize the fit, while domain knowledge may suggest a more meaningful combined feature.

Use a diagnosis-to-action loop

Write a specific hypothesis for each observed pattern. If residual spread increases with fitted values, ask whether target scale, missing interactions, or a genuine variance process explains it. If errors grow over time, check feature availability and measurement changes before immediately retraining. A plot should lead to a testable next experiment rather than a generic declaration that assumptions failed.

Keep the original evaluation population visible while iterating. Record the number of observations affected by any proposed rule and its consequence for model coverage. A system that abstains on some inputs may be useful, but it should report both error on accepted cases and the fraction of cases accepted. Quietly excluding difficult cases hides a material change in what the model promises to handle.

9. Interview questions

1. Is normality required to fit OLS?

No. The least-squares minimizer is algebraic. Normal conditional errors support particular exact inference results under additional assumptions.

2. Does heteroscedasticity necessarily bias coefficients?

Not if the conditional mean is correctly specified and error has zero conditional mean. It changes uncertainty and efficiency, and can invalidate conventional standard errors.

3. What is leverage?

A measure of an observation's position in design space, given by the corresponding hat-matrix diagonal. It differs from residual size.

4. Can robust standard errors fix confounding?

No. They address specified covariance problems, not a failure of zero conditional mean or causal identification.

5. Why inspect residuals over time?

Temporal ordering can reveal autocorrelation, regime changes, and drift hidden by aggregate plots.

6. Are fitted residuals independent under independent errors?

Generally no. The projection operation couples them and imposes constraints such as a zero sum when an intercept is fitted.

7. Should every extreme observation be removed?

No. Determine whether it is an error or a valid case. Deleting valid difficult cases can make the model inappropriate for deployment.

8. Does a clean residual plot prove the assumptions?

No. Diagnostics can reveal evidence against a model, but cannot certify every structural, sampling, or causal assumption.

10. Exercises and takeaways

  • Remove the quadratic component from the script while retaining changing noise variance. Identify which visual pattern remains.
  • Calculate the three-point hat matrix directly and verify its trace is two.
  • Create a grouped-data mini-project and compare random-row validation with validation on unseen groups.
  • Audit one real feature table for prediction-time availability, duplicate entities, and target-dependent exclusions before fitting a model.

Diagnostics are an investigation process. Match assumptions to the claim you want to make, inspect evidence at the right level, and avoid using a successful numerical fit as a substitute for a credible evaluation design.
