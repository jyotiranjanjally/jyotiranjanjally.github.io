1. A score is a choice about mistakes

A demand forecast can miss by one item on most days and by hundreds on a few holidays. A single average may hide that behaviour. Regression metrics summarize errors in different ways, and the choice should reflect the decision the model supports.

This tutorial derives MAE, MSE, RMSE, R², and adjusted R²; calculates them on one dataset; and explains constant-target and aggregation edge cases. You will also run an experiment that reports metrics from held-out predictions rather than training fits. Prerequisites are averages, squares, and the definition of a residual.

No metric alone measures fairness, calibration across every subgroup, or fitness for deployment. Pair summary scores with residual inspection, a relevant baseline, and error breakdowns in the units that stakeholders actually use.

2. Absolute distance versus squared distance

A residual eᵢ = yᵢ − y-hatᵢ is positive for underprediction and negative for overprediction. Averaging signed residuals measures bias but allows large positive and negative mistakes to cancel. MAE and MSE prevent this cancellation by transforming each residual before averaging.

\operatorname{MAE}=\frac1n\sum_i|e_i|,\qquad\operatorname{MSE}=\frac1n\sum_i e_i^2,\qquad\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_i e_i^2}

There are n evaluated observations. MAE is the mean absolute error in target units. MSE is mean squared error in squared target units. RMSE takes the square root after averaging, returning to target units while retaining sensitivity to large errors.

For an error of magnitude 10 instead of 1, the MAE contribution is ten times larger, while the MSE contribution is one hundred times larger. RMSE is not simply the average of per-observation square roots: that alternative would reduce to MAE.

3. What each loss rewards

For a constant prediction a, differentiating expected squared error gives 2(a − E[Y]), so the mean minimizes it when the relevant moments exist. For absolute error, the slope away from ties depends on how much probability lies below and above a. A median balances those probabilities and minimizes expected absolute loss.

RMSE is a strictly increasing transformation of MSE. On the same evaluation observations, minimizing either produces the same ranking of prediction vectors. This does not imply that averaging RMSE across folds is equivalent to taking the square root of pooled MSE; the square root is nonlinear.

\left(\frac1n\sum_i|e_i|\right)^2\leq\frac1n\sum_i e_i^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad\operatorname{MAE}\leq\operatorname{RMSE}

This inequality follows from Cauchy–Schwarz applied to the vector of absolute errors and a vector of ones. Equality occurs when all absolute errors are equal. A large gap between RMSE and MAE indicates variation in error magnitudes, but does not by itself identify the cause or prove a particular tail distribution.

4. R² and its boundary cases

R² compares residual squared error with the squared deviations of the evaluated targets from their own mean. Define SSE as the sum of squared prediction errors and SST as the total sum of squares around y-bar on the evaluation set.

R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{SSE}}{\mathrm{SST}}=1-\frac{\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}

For a nonconstant target vector, perfect predictions give 1. Predictions equal to the evaluation mean give 0. A value below zero means the predictions have greater squared error than that evaluation-mean reference. Negative R² is therefore valid and informative; there is no general lower bound.

The evaluation mean is a descriptive reference computed from the evaluated targets, not a deployable model available before observing them. A practical mean baseline uses the training mean and can itself receive negative test R². Keep this distinction clear when comparing to DummyRegressor.

When all evaluation targets are constant, SST is zero and the mathematical ratio is undefined. Scikit-learn's default force_finite=True replaces perfect constant-target cases with 1 and imperfect ones with 0 for practical model-search behaviour. With finite coercion disabled, perfect cases produce NaN and imperfect cases negative infinity. With fewer than two samples, R² is not a meaningful variance-relative statistic.

5. Adjusted R² is a different statistic

\bar R^2=1-(1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}

Here p is the number of non-intercept predictors and n is the sample count. This conventional adjusted R² applies to an in-sample OLS fit with an intercept and positive residual degrees of freedom, n > p + 1. It compares variance estimates after accounting for the fitted predictor count. It is not the same as held-out R² and is not a substitute for validation.

Adding a predictor cannot decrease the best in-sample OLS R², but it can decrease adjusted R² if the fit improvement is insufficient. For regularized models, simply inserting the number of columns ignores their effective degrees of freedom. Do not label that plug-in quantity as a universally valid complexity adjustment.

6. Calculate every metric

Let actual targets be [1,2,3] and predictions [1,2,5]. Residuals are [0,0,−2], absolute errors [0,0,2], and squared errors [0,0,4]. Therefore MAE = 2/3, MSE = 4/3, and RMSE = √(4/3), approximately 1.155.

The target mean is 2. Its deviations are [−1,0,1], giving SST = 2. Thus R² = 1 − 4/2 = −1. The prediction vector is worse under squared error than the constant evaluation-mean prediction [2,2,2], whose SSE is 2.

For a separate fitted OLS example with n = 10, p = 2, and R² = 0.8, adjusted R² = 1 − 0.2·9/7, approximately 0.743. The adjustment is meaningful only under the stated setting; it should not be applied to the arbitrary three-point prediction vector just because both are available numbers.

7. Change one large error

Increasing one residual makes MSE rise quadratically while MAE rises linearly. R² becomes increasingly negative as SSE exceeds the fixed target variation. This example holds the target distribution fixed, isolating the effect of an error rather than mixing it with changes in dataset difficulty.

8. Compute metrics transparently

The script trains on one subset, predicts another, computes metrics from NumPy operations, and verifies agreement with scikit-learn. It also plots actual against predicted values. The diagonal represents perfect predictions; distance from it exposes both systematic and occasional errors.

Scikit-learn scoring names for losses often begin with neg_ because model selection maximizes a score. A negative MSE score is not a negative squared error: it is the library's sign convention. Negate it when reporting loss, and take care when converting negative mean squared scores into RMSE.

9. Evaluate the evaluation

Compare models on the same rows and target scale. RMSE measured in dollars cannot be compared directly with RMSE measured in thousands of dollars. R² is dimensionless but still depends on the variation of the particular target sample, so it does not make unrelated datasets equally difficult.

For grouped observations, report both observation-weighted and group-level views when relevant. A large customer can dominate an overall score and hide poor behaviour for smaller customers. If observations have unequal decision costs, use a justified sample-weight policy and document it.

For time series, inspect rolling performance and seasonality. Repeatedly looking at a test set while revising features turns it into a validation set. Model selection, threshold or clipping choices, and target transformations must all be finalized before the final test report.

Understand weighted errors and aggregation

If observation i has a nonnegative weight wᵢ, a weighted mean loss divides the weighted sum by the total weight, not by the row count. The weights should represent a documented population or decision rule. Giving difficult cases smaller weights after seeing their errors is a way to manipulate a report, not a principled evaluation method.

\operatorname{MSE}_w=\frac{\sum_iw_i e_i^2}{\sum_iw_i},\qquad \sum_iw_i>0

For errors [1,3] and weights [3,1], weighted MSE = (3·1 + 1·9)/4 = 3 and weighted RMSE = √3. Unweighted MSE is 5. Neither is intrinsically more truthful; they answer differently weighted questions. State the weighting policy next to the score so readers know which population or cost distribution it represents.

Pooling matters across folds too. Suppose one fold has one observation with MSE 1 and another has nine observations with MSE 9. The equally weighted mean fold MSE is 5, but pooled MSE is (1·1 + 9·9)/10 = 8.2. Mean fold RMSE is (1 + 3)/2 = 2, while pooled RMSE is √8.2, approximately 2.864. A report must identify its aggregation rather than using the label RMSE for all of these quantities.

Measure uncertainty without reusing the test set for selection

A score from a finite test sample is an estimate, not a fixed property of an algorithm. A paired bootstrap can resample the same test-row indices for two fixed prediction vectors and summarize the variability of their metric difference. Pairing preserves the fact that both models were evaluated on the same cases.

The resampling unit must respect dependence. If several rows belong to one customer, resampling independent rows can understate variability relative to resampling customers. Time-series dependence needs a suitable block or temporal approach. A confidence interval from the wrong sampling model does not become reliable merely because many bootstrap repetitions were used.

For a fixed-model test bootstrap, do not retrain the model inside every resample unless the goal is explicitly to evaluate training-process variability. Conversely, a fixed-prediction bootstrap does not capture uncertainty from selecting features and hyperparameters. Specify which uncertainty source the procedure measures.

Avoid misleading percentage summaries

Percentage errors can explode when actual targets approach zero and can become hard to interpret for negative values. Even though this tutorial focuses on MAE and squared-error metrics, a request for “percentage accuracy” should trigger clarification of the formula and target domain. Regression has no universal accuracy percentage analogous to the fraction of correctly classified labels.

10. Interview questions

1. Why can R² be negative?

The prediction SSE can exceed the target's sum of squares around its evaluation mean. The formula then yields a value below zero.

2. Which metric shares the target's units?

MAE and RMSE do. MSE has squared units; R² is dimensionless.

3. Can MAE exceed RMSE on the same equally weighted data?

No. Cauchy–Schwarz gives MAE ≤ RMSE, with equality when all absolute residuals have the same magnitude.

4. Does RMSE choose a different best model than MSE?

Not on the same pooled prediction vector, because square root preserves ordering. Different fold aggregation can alter rankings.

5. What happens for constant targets?

The mathematical R² denominator is zero. Library finite-value conventions must be distinguished from that mathematical undefined case.

6. Why can a mean dummy model have negative test R²?

It predicts the training mean, while test R² uses the test mean as its reference. Those means need not coincide.

7. Is adjusted R² a test metric?

It is conventionally an in-sample OLS complexity adjustment. Held-out evaluation answers a different generalization question.

8. Which loss is preferable for rare large failures?

Squared loss emphasizes them more strongly, but the decision should reflect actual cost. Also report tail errors or operational failure rates so the average does not hide the event of interest.

11. Exercises and takeaways

  • Calculate all metrics for predictions [2,2,2] on targets [1,2,3], then compare with the worked example.
  • Construct two prediction vectors where one wins on MAE and the other on RMSE. Explain the distribution of residual magnitudes.
  • Compare pooled RMSE with mean fold RMSE using unequal fold sizes.
  • Create an error-report mini-project showing a baseline, units, subgroup counts, and actual-versus-predicted plots.

A metric compresses a distribution of mistakes. Choose it deliberately, preserve its units and conventions, and inspect the errors that its average can hide.
